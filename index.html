LESSON 1

What GTO means

Game theory optimal play is a Nash equilibrium: a pair of strategies where neither player can win more by changing only their own. If you play it, no opponent strategy can beat you in the long run. The best they can do is break even against you, minus the mistakes they make.

That is the key trade. GTO is unexploitable, not maximally profitable. Against a player who never bluffs, folding your bluff-catchers earns more than the equilibrium does. That kind of adjustment is called exploitative play.

Solvers find equilibria for simplified games: fixed stack depths, a menu of two or three bet sizes, set ranges going in. Their output is a map, not the territory. The goal of study is to learn the reasons behind the map, so you can rebuild good decisions at the table without memorizing trees.

  • Think in ranges. You never play one hand. You play every hand you'd hold in this spot.
  • Make opponents indifferent. Balanced bet and bluff ratios leave them no profitable counter.
  • Protect weak spots. If you always check your weak hands, your checks become easy to attack.
LESSON 2

Pot odds

When an opponent bets B into a pot of P, you risk B to win P + B. The call breaks even when your equity equals your share of the final pot.

equity needed = B ÷ (P + 2B)
Try it

Common sizes are worth memorizing: a third of the pot needs 20%, half pot 25%, two-thirds 28.6%, pot 33.3%, and a 2x overbet 40%.

Before the river, raw pot odds understate some hands and overstate others. Draws to the nuts can win extra later (implied odds). Weak hands out of position often can't realize their full equity because they fold to later bets.

LESSON 3

Defense and bluffing math

Three numbers come from the same bet size, and solvers lean on all of them.

  • Fold equity a pure bluff needs: B ÷ (P + B). A pot-size bluff with no equity must work half the time.
  • Minimum defense frequency (MDF): P ÷ (P + B). Fold more than this and the bettor profits by betting any two cards.
  • Bluff share of a balanced river bet: B ÷ (P + 2B). This mixes bluffs so the caller's bluff-catchers break even. It equals the caller's pot odds.
Bet size explorer

Bigger bets need fewer folds per bet to show a profit, but each one asks for more folds as a share of the time. They also let you carry more bluffs, because the caller is getting a worse price. That is why polarized ranges like big sizes.

MDF is strictest on the river. On earlier streets you can fold a little more than MDF because some of your continuing hands will have to fold later anyway, and the bettor's bluffs still have equity.

LESSON 4

Combos and blockers

There are 1,326 starting hands. Each pocket pair has 6 combos, each suited hand 4, and each offsuit hand 12. So AK (16 combos) is far more common than AA (6).

Cards you can see remove combos from your opponent's range. That is card removal, and the cards doing it are called blockers. Holding the A♠ cuts AA from 6 combos to 3 and AK from 16 to 12. It's why A5s is a favourite 3-bet bluff: it makes it less likely the opponent has the hands that would 4-bet you.

LESSON 5

Ranges and position

Opening ranges widen as fewer players are left to act. From UTG five players can wake up with a strong hand, so the solver opens about 18% of hands. From the button only the blinds remain, and it opens over 40%.

Position matters because the player who acts last sees more before deciding. They realize more of their equity: they get to check back marginal hands, take free cards and bluff with better information. Hands like suited connectors gain the most from position and lose the most without it.

Facing a raise, your range splits three ways: hands strong enough to 3-bet for value, hands that play well as a call, and a slice of bluffs, often suited aces and suited connectors that block strong hands and play well when called. Open the range charts and compare BB against UTG with BB against the button.

LESSON 6

Polarized vs merged ranges

A polarized betting range holds very strong hands and bluffs, with medium hands checked. It suits large bets and overbets, especially on the river, where medium hands gain little from betting.

A merged (or linear) range bets strong and medium hands together. It suits small bets, used when the opponent has many weak hands that can call a small bet but not a big one.

Which one the solver picks comes down to two advantages. Range advantage: whose range has more equity overall on this board. Nut advantage: who has more of the very best hands. A big nut advantage points to big bets. A range advantage without the nuts points to frequent small bets, like the preflop raiser betting a third of the pot on A-7-2 rainbow.

LESSON 7

Mixed strategies

Charts show some hands 3-betting 50% and calling 50%. That's no glitch. At equilibrium, a hand that mixes earns the same EV with each action. The solver splits between them because doing only one would unbalance the range and give the opponent something to exploit.

For a human, two lessons follow. First, mixed hands are the ones where getting it "wrong" costs the least, so don't agonize over them. Second, if you want to mix, use a random trigger such as the second hand on a watch (0–29 call, 30–59 raise) instead of your mood.

Many players simplify: they pick the action with the higher frequency and play it every time. That gives up a little EV and is a fine way to start.

LESSON 8

When to deviate

GTO is a baseline that protects you when you don't know your opponent. Once you have evidence, move away from it on purpose:

  • Opponent calls too much: bluff less, value-bet thinner and larger.
  • Opponent folds too much: bluff more often, especially with small bets.
  • Opponent rarely bluffs big rivers: fold more bluff-catchers than MDF says.
  • Opponent opens too wide: 3-bet more for value and as bluffs.

The risk is that each exploit opens a hole in your own strategy. Exploit what you've seen, and drift back toward balance when the opponent adjusts. A good study loop: learn the baseline here, drill it on the practice page, then review real hands on the review page.
